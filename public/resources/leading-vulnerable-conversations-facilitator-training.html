Starting Point Men · Facilitator Training Curriculum

Leading Vulnerable Conversations

A four-session training for men’s group facilitators at Starting Point Church, built on the Step-by-Step Practice for Facilitating Vulnerable Conversation from Chapter 5 of Building Authentic Men’s Ministries for Today’s Church by John S. Lybarger, Ph.D.

4 sessions · 90 minutes each Field practice between sessions For table leaders, group facilitators, mentors

Why This Training Exists

“One of the most common challenges men face in building brotherhood is not knowing how to move conversation from surface to depth.”Chapter 5, Step-by-Step Practice: Facilitating Vulnerable Conversation

Starting Point exists to help people “seek Jesus, not religion.” It welcomes the non-churched, the de-churched, and displaced Christians, and it says plainly that “it’s OK to have a lot of questions along the way.” Men who come from those places will not open up because a leader tells them to. They open up when a facilitator makes it safe and shows them how. Chapter 2 puts it this way: men remove their masks “when someone else demonstrates that it’s safe to do so.”

This curriculum trains facilitators in six learnable skills so every Starting Point men’s table, Discovery Group, and mentoring pair can move from surface talk to real brotherhood.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the training, each facilitator will be able to:

  • Set up and maintain a confidentiality covenant, including its limits.
  • Prepare and share a calibrated personal disclosure that gives other men permission to be honest.
  • Ask open-ended questions that invite progressively deeper sharing without pressure.
  • Respond to disclosure with presence and validation instead of advice.
  • Match another man’s vulnerability appropriately without hijacking the conversation.
  • Follow up in a way that shows men they were heard and are cared for over time.
  • Recognize when a situation goes beyond peer support and make a referral.

1

Create Safety Through Confidentiality

Session 1
“Before any vulnerable sharing, establish explicit confidentiality: ‘What we share here stays here. We don’t repeat each other’s stories, even to our wives, without permission.’ This boundary creates psychological safety necessary for honesty.”Chapter 5

Core Teaching

Men will not risk honesty until they believe it is safe. Chapter 2 explains that the physical space “creates the container, but relational agreements establish the culture.” Every Starting Point men’s group begins with the Group Covenant, which defines confidentiality, states its limits, and names consequences for breaches.

  • Be specific. Many men assume they can share prayer requests or group content with their wives. The covenant says clearly that they can’t without permission.
  • State the limits. “Confidentiality will be maintained except in cases where someone is in immediate danger of harming themselves or others, or where abuse of a minor is disclosed.”
  • Repeat it. Open every gathering with “As always, what’s shared here stays here.”
  • Model it. Leaders never use a member’s story as an illustration, prayer request, or example without explicit permission. One violation by a leader can destroy trust that took months to build.

Example

Weak opening

“Okay guys, you know the drill, keep it confidential. Who wants to share?”

Vague, assumed, and rushed. Men don’t know what “confidential” covers.

Strong opening

“Before we start, a reminder: what we share here stays here. That means we don’t repeat anyone’s story, even to our wives, unless he gives permission. The one exception is if someone is in danger. Then we get help. Everyone good with that?”

Explicit, includes the limit, and invites agreement.

Activity 1A: The Confidentiality Conversation25 minutes · whole group, then triads

From the Chapter 2 coaching exercise. The trainer leads this with the whole group first, then each facilitator practices leading it in a triad.

  1. Ask: “Think about a time when someone betrayed your trust by sharing something you told them in confidence. You don’t need to share the details, but how did that betrayal affect you? How did it change your willingness to be vulnerable?” Let several men respond.
  2. Ask: “Now imagine the opposite, a relationship where you knew, without doubt, that anything you shared would be protected. How would that safety change what you’re willing to talk about?”
  3. Say: “This covenant isn’t just a piece of paper. It’s a sacred promise we’re making to each other… Can you make that promise?” Have each man verbally affirm his commitment before signing.
  4. In triads, each man takes a turn leading steps 1–3 in his own words. Observers give one “keep” and one “try” note.
Activity 1B: Space Audit Walk-Through15 minutes · on site

Walk through your meeting room as if you were a first-time guest, using the Chapter 2 Space Audit:

  • Can conversations be overheard from outside? Have someone speak at normal volume while you listen from the hallway.
  • Does the seating create connection? Circles and ovals promote equality; rows and tables create distance.
  • What does the lighting communicate: institution or hospitality?
  • Is there visual privacy? Can blinds be closed?
  • Can men arrive and leave without walking through public areas?

Write down three changes you can make within the next month.

Step 1 Facilitator Checklist

Common Pitfalls

  • Assuming men know what confidentiality means
  • Skipping the reminder once the group “feels safe”
  • Leaders sharing a man’s story in a sermon, prayer chain, or leader meeting with identifying details
  • Meeting in a room where hallway traffic can hear

Reflection Questions

  1. When has someone broken your trust, and how did it change what you were willing to share afterward?
  2. What would make a skeptical or de-churched man believe your group is truly safe?
  3. Is there any habit of yours (prayer chains, talking with your wife, leader updates) that could accidentally break confidentiality?
2

Model Vulnerability First

Session 2
“Don’t ask others to be vulnerable if you’re not willing to go first. Share something real about your own struggle or failure. Your vulnerability gives others permission to be honest.”Chapter 5

Core Teaching

Chapter 2 calls this strategic vulnerability: “the intentional practice of going first, going deep, and going real to create permission for others to follow.” It “isn’t emotional exhibitionism or therapeutic oversharing; it’s a carefully calibrated leadership practice.”

The facilitator sets the depth of the room. If he shares a polished testimony, men will polish theirs. If he shares something real, and it’s received with grace, men learn that honesty is safe here. This reflects Starting Point’s conviction that “we all do it [sin]… and, even then, God will love us and passionately pursue us.”

Example: The Facilitator Goes First (from Chapter 2)

Facilitator: “Before we go around the circle, I’ll start. One thing going well: my daughter and I have been having breakfast together every Saturday… One challenge I’m facing: I’ve been struggling with anxiety about a work situation that feels out of my control, and it’s affecting my sleep. One thing I’m afraid to say out loud: I’m afraid that if I don’t perform perfectly at work, I’ll lose my value, not just my job, but my worth as a person.”

Pause. Let the disclosure settle. Make eye contact around the circle.

Facilitator: “Okay, who wants to go next? Remember, you can share at whatever depth feels right for you.”

Too safe

“I guess my struggle is I need to be better about reading my Bible.”

True, but sanitized. It signals that the room is for tidy answers.

Real and calibrated

“Honestly, I’ve been short with my kids all week, and I think it’s because I’m anxious about money. I haven’t told my wife how worried I am.”

Specific, current, and honest without being graphic or unprocessed.

Activity 2A: Build Your 90-Second Share20 minutes · individual, then pairs
  1. On an index card, write your own answers to the Three-Question Framework: one thing going well, one current challenge, and one thing you’re afraid to say out loud.
  2. Check your share with the calibration test below.
  3. Share it aloud with a partner in 90 seconds or less. Partner responds only with, “Thank you for trusting me with that.”
  4. Switch roles. Then discuss: What did it feel like to go first? What did it feel like to hear it?
Calibration TestAsk Yourself
RealIs this a genuine, current struggle, not a polished past victory?
ProcessedAm I stable enough with this to share it without needing the group to carry me through it tonight?
PurposefulDoes this share make room for others, or does it center me?
AppropriateIs it free of graphic detail and of other people’s private information (for example, my wife’s story)?

Step 2 Facilitator Checklist

Common Pitfalls

  • The sanitized testimony that only describes past victories
  • Oversharing raw or unprocessed pain that leaves the group caring for the leader
  • Sharing someone else’s story, including a spouse’s, without permission
  • Going first, then talking for ten minutes

Reflection Questions

  1. What do you fear will happen if the men in your group see your real struggles?
  2. Who modeled vulnerability for you, and what did it give you permission to do?
  3. What is one area where you are tempted to share the polished version instead of the real one?
3

Use Open-Ended Questions

Session 2
“Move beyond yes/no questions to open-ended invitations: ‘What’s been weighing on you lately?’ ‘Where are you struggling right now?’ ‘What would you want prayer for if you were being completely honest?’ ‘What’s one thing you haven’t told anyone?’”Chapter 5

Core Teaching

Closed questions produce short answers and let men stay on the surface. Open-ended questions invite a story. Chapter 2’s Three-Question Framework sequences questions so depth grows gradually and men keep a sense of control:

QuestionWhy It Works
1. “What’s one thing going well in your life right now?”Starts from strength. Men relax when they can begin from a position of competence.
2. “What’s one challenge you’re currently facing?”“One challenge,” not “all your problems.” The bounded invitation makes vulnerability manageable.
3. “What’s one thing you’re afraid to say out loud?”“This is where masks begin to crack.” Naming the fear gives permission for it to be voiced.

Never pressure disclosure. Some men need to witness others’ vulnerability before risking their own. “That’s not resistance; that’s wisdom.” Leave the invitation open: “If anyone wants to share what came to mind, we’re here to listen without judgment.”

Welcome silence. Notice your own anxiety when silence follows a question, and resist filling it. “Silence is often where the deepest insights emerge.”

Activity 3A: Question Makeover15 minutes · pairs

Rewrite each closed question as an open-ended one. Compare with the sample answers after you try.

Closed QuestionOpen-Ended Invitation
“Is everything good at home?”“What’s life at home been like this week?”
“Did you read your Bible this week?”“Where have you sensed God, or felt His absence, this week?”
“Are you stressed about work?”“What’s been weighing on you lately?”
“Anything we can pray about?”“What would you want prayer for if you were being completely honest?”
“Do you and your son get along?”“How would you describe where things are with your son right now?”
Activity 3B: Three-Question Triads20 minutes · triads (facilitator, sharer, observer)
  1. The facilitator asks the three questions in order. After each answer he asks one clarifying question: “Can you say more about that?” or “What’s that been like for you?”
  2. He practices soft eye contact, leans slightly forward, and resists interrupting or offering solutions.
  3. The observer notes any closed questions, advice, or rushed silence.
  4. Rotate roles so everyone facilitates once. Debrief: What felt safe? What made you more or less willing to go deeper?

Depth Ladder

Chapter 5’s Vulnerability Assessment describes five levels of sharing. Use it to notice where your group is and to invite the next level gently, not to grade men.

LevelWhat It Sounds Like
1Surface conversation only (weather, sports, work logistics)
2Occasional sharing of opinions or preferences
3Sharing challenges or struggles in general terms
4Sharing specific struggles and asking for support
5Complete transparency about failures, fears, and needs

Step 3 Facilitator Checklist

Common Pitfalls

  • Stacking several questions at once
  • Leading questions that contain the “right” answer
  • Jumping to the deepest question in the first meeting
  • Answering your own question when the silence gets uncomfortable

Reflection Questions

  1. Which question would be hardest for you to answer honestly, and why?
  2. How comfortable are you with 10 seconds of silence in a group? What do you usually do with it?
  3. At what level of the depth ladder is your group now? What would a gentle next step look like?
4

Listen Without Fixing

Session 3
“When someone shares vulnerability, resist the urge to immediately problem-solve. Practice saying: ‘Thank you for trusting me with that’ or ‘That sounds really hard’ or ‘Tell me more about that.’ Your presence and validation are more valuable than your solutions.”Chapter 5

Core Teaching

Many men are wired to solve problems. When a brother shares pain, advice can feel like help, but it often tells him, “Your feelings are a problem to get rid of.” Quick fixes shut down further sharing. Presence says, “You’re not alone, and you don’t have to have it figured out.” That is the grace Starting Point wants every man to experience.

Presence phrases to practice:

  • “Thank you for trusting me with that.”
  • “That sounds really hard.”
  • “Tell me more about that.”
  • “What’s that been like for you?”
  • “Anyone else relate to that?”

Example

Fixing

Man: “My marriage feels distant. We’re like roommates.”

Leader: “You need a date night. My wife and I do Fridays. Also read Love and Respect.”

Fast, well-meaning, and it ends the conversation.

Listening (from Chapter 2)

Man: “My marriage feels pretty distant right now. We’re like roommates managing logistics, not partners.”

Facilitator: “Thank you for that honesty. That takes courage. Can you say more about what ‘distant’ feels like in your marriage?”

Later: “That loneliness in the midst of a full house, that’s a painful place to be. Anyone else relate to that?”

Activity 4A: The Fix-It Tally25 minutes · triads
  1. The sharer describes a real, moderate challenge for three minutes (for example, a work conflict or a parenting frustration).
  2. The listener may use only presence phrases and open-ended questions. No advice, no stories, no Scripture “answers.”
  3. The observer keeps a tally of every fix attempt, including disguised ones (“Have you thought about…?”).
  4. After three minutes, the sharer answers: “When did you feel most heard? Was there a moment you felt managed?”
  5. Rotate. Then discuss as a group: What made it hard not to fix?

Advice has a place, but only when invited. Teach facilitators to ask, “Would it help to hear what’s worked for me, or do you just need us to listen tonight?”

Activity 4B: The Silence Drill15 minutes · pairs

After the sharer finishes, the listener counts silently to five before saying anything. Repeat three times. Debrief: What did the silence make room for?

Step 4 Facilitator Checklist

Common Pitfalls

  • Quick Bible-verse answers that close the conversation
  • “At least…” statements that minimize pain
  • Checking a phone or looking away while a man is sharing
  • Letting the group pile on with advice

Reflection Questions

  1. When did someone listen to you without fixing? How did it affect you?
  2. What is your usual “fix” reflex (advice, Scripture, a story, humor)? What need of yours does it serve?
  3. How might Jesus’ way of asking questions shape the way you listen?
5

Reciprocate Appropriately

Session 3
“If someone shares vulnerability, reciprocate with similar depth. If he shares about marriage struggles, don’t respond with a story about your golf game. Match his vulnerability with your own, creating mutual trust.”Chapter 5

Core Teaching

When a man takes a risk and nobody meets him there, he feels exposed and alone. When someone responds with similar depth, the risk becomes shared and trust grows. Reciprocity is about matching depth, not matching story. The goal is “me too,” not “me more.”

  • Match the level. Respond at about the same level of the depth ladder.
  • Keep it short. Share briefly, then return the focus to him.
  • Don’t one-up. Your story should say “you’re not alone,” not “mine was worse.”
  • Don’t retreat. Humor or a subject change after a deep share leaves him hanging.

Example

Mismatched

Man: “I’ve been struggling with pornography again, and I’m ashamed.”

Other man: “Yeah, we all have stuff. Hey, did anyone catch the game?”

Retreat to the surface. The man who took the risk is left exposed.

Matched

Other man: “Thank you for saying that. I know that shame. I fought the same battle for years, and what finally helped was telling someone. You’re not the only one in this room.”

Similar depth, brief, and turns the focus back to hope and brotherhood.

Activity 5A: Match the Depth20 minutes · small groups

For each disclosure, write one response that matches its depth and one that misses. Discuss why.

  1. “Since the divorce, weekends without my kids are the loneliest days I’ve ever had.”
  2. “I lost my job two months ago, and I haven’t told anyone at church.”
  3. “Since my wife died, I don’t know who I am anymore.”
  4. “I don’t even know if I believe in God. I just come because my wife wants me to.”

Note for scenario 4: Starting Point welcomes doubt. A matched response might be, “I’m really glad you said that. I’ve had seasons where I wasn’t sure either. Honest questions are welcome here.”

Step 5 Facilitator Checklist

Common Pitfalls

  • Hijacking the moment with a long personal story
  • Going much deeper than the sharer and making him uncomfortable
  • Nervous jokes that break the moment
  • Silence from the group that feels like judgment

Reflection Questions

  1. When have you shared something real and had no one meet you there? What did you conclude?
  2. What is the difference between “me too” and “me more” in your own conversations?
  3. What story from your life could help another man feel less alone, and when would it be wise to share it?
6

Follow Up

Session 4
“After someone shares something significant, follow up in your next conversation: ‘How are you doing with that situation you shared last time?’ This communicates that you were actually listening and that you care about his ongoing journey, not just the moment of disclosure.”Chapter 5

Core Teaching

Vulnerability is a moment; brotherhood is a journey. Follow-up turns a disclosure into a relationship. It also protects men after a vulnerability hangover, the second-guessing that often comes the day after sharing something real.

  • Within a few days: A short text or call after a significant share.
  • At the next gathering: A private check-in, never putting him on the spot in front of the group.
  • Keep notes carefully: If you jot reminders, use no identifying details and keep them private.
  • Notice absence: When a man stops showing up, reach out. His silence may be a signal.

Example Messages

Text after a significant share

“Hey brother, I’ve been praying for you since Thursday. Thanks again for trusting us. No need to reply, just wanted you to know.”

Next-gathering check-in (private)

“How are you doing with that situation you shared last time?”

Public follow-up

“So Mike, update us all on the marriage thing.”

Puts him on the spot and treats his story as group property.

Reaching out after absence (from Chapter 5)

“I know we don’t know each other well, but I’m concerned about you. Would you be willing to meet for coffee? No agenda, I just want to check in.”

Activity 6A: Follow-Up Plan Card10 minutes · individual

Think of the men in your group, or the men you expect to lead. On an index card, write:

  1. The name or initials of one man who shared something significant recently
  2. How you will follow up (text, call, coffee) and by what day
  3. The exact words you will use
  4. How you will pray for him this week

Keep the card private and destroy it once you’ve followed up.

Step 6 Facilitator Checklist

Common Pitfalls

  • Forgetting what a man shared, which tells him it didn’t matter
  • Following up publicly instead of privately
  • Accepting “just busy” when your gut says something is wrong
  • Carrying every man alone instead of involving other brothers and the care team

Reflection Questions

  1. When did someone follow up with you after a hard season? What did it communicate?
  2. What system will you use so follow-up doesn’t depend on memory?
  3. Who is one man you should check on this week?

Case Study: The Brotherhood That Saved a Life

Summarized from Chapter 5. Use in Session 4 (20 minutes).

Nick attended his church’s men’s breakfast for three years and considered himself “involved.” When his marriage began falling apart and he began having suicidal thoughts, he didn’t call anyone. He sat alone in his car in a parking garage.

Three men from that breakfast, James, Albert, and Steven, had been meeting weekly in a small brotherhood group for a year, sharing their real struggles. When Nick stopped attending, James noticed and texted him. Nick replied, “Yeah, just busy. Work stuff.” James had learned not to accept surface answers when his gut said something was wrong, so he called: “I know we don’t know each other well, but I’m concerned about you. Would you be willing to meet for coffee?”

Over coffee James asked, “How are you really doing?” Nick broke down. James responded, “Thank you for trusting me with this. You’re not alone. I’ve been where you are, and I have brothers who’ve walked with me through it. Will you let us walk with you?” The brothers welcomed Nick, met with him weekly, helped him find a Christian therapist, encouraged him to see his doctor, and walked with him through marriage counseling.

Discussion Questions

  1. Three years of breakfasts did not create a place where Nick could be honest. What was missing?
  2. Find each of the six steps in James’s actions. Which was most important?
  3. How did James’s own brotherhood group prepare him for this moment?
  4. James combined brotherhood with professional care. Why does that matter?
  5. Who at Starting Point might be sitting in the parking lot right now? What would it take to notice him?

Boundaries and Referral

Facilitators are brothers and guides, not therapists. Chapter 6 describes a mental-health-aware ministry as one that combines “spiritual support combined with appropriate professional referral, compassion without overstepping boundaries.” It models the pastor’s response: “I’m not equipped to be your therapist. What I can do is walk with you, pray with you, and help you find the professional help you need.”

If a man is in immediate danger of harming himself or someone else, or if abuse of a minor is disclosed, confidentiality does not apply. Stay with him, call 911 in an emergency, and contact the pastoral team.

For suicidal thoughts or emotional crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline).

Signs a conversation needs more than the group
  • Talk of suicide, hopelessness, or “everyone would be better off without me”
  • Disclosure of abuse, violence, or a threat to someone’s safety
  • Addiction that is escalating or affecting work or family
  • Trauma symptoms, persistent depression, or anxiety that disrupts daily life
  • Marriage crisis involving separation, infidelity, or safety concerns

When in doubt, consult the pastoral team or the Care and Recovery Lead and use Starting Point’s referral list. Faith, therapy, and community work together; seeking counseling is not a failure of faith.
